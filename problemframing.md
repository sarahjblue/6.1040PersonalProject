# Problem Framing

## Domain

Clothing reuse on MIT campus. Collectively, college students own large amounts of clothing. When students are ready to get rid of that clothing, multiple options exist, such as throwing clothing out, textile recycling, reselling, or donating. Some students participate in clothing donations or resale, but many opt to simply throw their clothing out because it is easier and requires no additional time, effort, or information. Some students try to resell their clothing to peers via DormSpam (mass emails to the MIT student body), and some students donate unused clothing to spring clothing collections. The stakeholders in the process are largely the students themselves. as well as student clubs such as UA Sustain and Infinite Threads, but the environment and people around the world are impacted by textile waste. Students are impacted by their ability to easily buy, sell, and get rid of clothing. Depending on what happens to these textiles, the environment and people around the world bear the impacts, if the textile items end up in landfills or shipped overseas. UA Sustain and Infinite Threads aim to help students reuse clothing and think about their environmental impact.

## Bad Situation

There is no easy, efficient, and consistently accessible way to buy, sell, or recycle clothing for students at MIT.

## Corrobation

### Clothing Waste (in general + on campus)
Clothing reuse on college campuses is part of a greater domain that is textile reuse. The EPA (Environmental Protection Agency) reports that Americans generate 17 million tons of textile waste per year ([Green America](https://greenamerica.org/unraveling-fashion-industry/what-really-happens-unwanted-clothes)). About 2.5 million tons of that clothing gets recycled, but more than 3 million tons are incinerated and some 11 million tons are sent to landfills. This problem is especially prevalent on college campuses, even more so during spring move outs, when students may not want to pack up and move their belongings. According to Tufts, in 2015, students left behind 230 tons of waste between May and June ([Planet Aid](https://www.planetaid.org/blog/curbing-the-college-waste-problem)). With around 12,000 undergrad students, this is approximately 38 1/3 pounds of waste per student, with much of this waste being textile or other reusable goods. In the fiscal year 2019, George Washington University collected 48.7 tons of clothing ([GWU](https://drawdown.psu.edu/sites/default/files/posters/clothes-are-not-trash-clothing-recovery-college-campus-nations-capital.pdf)). Here at MIT, thousands of clothing items and other textiles are collected each spring during move out. In the academic year 2025-2026, one of MIT's student ran clubs, infinite threads, resold over 700 articles of clothing and sent numerous pounds of textile waste to textile recycling.

### Clothing Reuse (in general + on campus)
One of the main ways to address clothing waste is through thrifting and other second hand use. In 2021, Goodwill received over 107 million pieces of donated items, amounting to about 5.7 billion pounds ([Threads Monthly](https://threadsmonthly.com/clothing-donation-stats/)). This donation process is not as positive as many assume, with an estimated 85% of donated clothing with the intent of being reused, heading to the landfill or being incinerated. At MIT, there is a spring clothing collection drive every spring hosted by Infinite Threads, a sub-team of UA Sustain. This clothing is then sorted, and either sold to students in thrift pop-ups or moved to a textile recycling process.

## Workarounds

### DormSpam Workaround
One workaround MIT students currently use to mitigate clothing waste is to resell clothing items on DormSpam ([DormSpam Definition: read page 1](https://studentlife.mit.edu/app/uploads/2025/05/20240729-dormspam-wg-rev16-final.docx.pdf)). One issue with this resale option is that many students do not opt in to DormSpam for various reasons, such as the up to hundreds of emails received each day, which can inevitably clog an email. With many students not opting into DormSpam, the opportunity for clothing reuse is limited to those who do opt in, from both the seller and buyer end. Another reason that the DormSpam workaround is inconvient is that these emails can often get lost in inboxes. In the last three weeks, there has been roughly one "move in/move out sale" dormspam email a day. These come along with nearly 50 emails a day via dormspam depending on time of year. This format overwhelms inboxes and many emails get deleted immediately.

### Facebook Marketplace/similar platforms
Another workaround that MIT students can use to mitigate clothing waste is to resell items on Facebook Marketplace. This option, however, opens MIT students to the greater Boston area, and many may want a closer, smaller network. Working with to sell and buy items with people from the greater Boston area exposes students to more time consuming processes (aligning schedules, travel, etc), more people (can leave individuals feeling unsafe), and requires students to create a facebook profile (students voice concerns about time spent on social media, privacy concerns, and more). According to New York Times, "In interviews, young people said that they saw the apps as valuable spaces to gather, as well as a waste of time" ([New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/26/style/teens-meta-instagram-facebook-restrictions.html)). Individuals who are working to avoid social media will not utilize Facebook Marketplace and other social media platforms that have resale options because of the main reason for the platform.

### Clothing Thrift Pop-ups
One other workaround that MIT students use to mitigate clothing waste is to donate their clothing during MIT's spring clothing collection, ran by Infinite Threads, an MIT student club. These collected items are then sorted and resold throughout the fall and spring semester. This option is great for internal reuse. One problem with this solution is that MIT students are unaware of when and how to donate items, or do not wish to wait until spring. Another issue that students may have with this issue is that they themselves wish to sell and profit from the sale of their clothing.

## Solution Sketch
My solution is a web application where users can upload images of an item that they wish to put up for sale or offer for free, designed originally for MIT campus. The user will be able to upload an image of the item and add text details such as quality. Users will be able to message one another about items for conversations such as asking questions, making offers, and finalizing sales. In the future, the web application may also have a mobile app counterpart. If the application expands beyond MIT, students will be grouped by their campus to keep sales an internal operation. Within the application, there will also be a way to provide students with information about other ways to discard their belongings (ie: textile recycling drop offs, school-wide collections).


## Stakeholders

- Students: Students play the largest role in this problem because in some ways, they are both the problem and the solution. Students need a way to discard of textile goods that they no longer wish to own. Students also need a way to purchase new to them textile goods in an easy, efficient, cost effective way.

- Environment: the environment pays the largest cost for textile waste. The fashion industry produces 10% of the global carbon emissions ([GWU](https://drawdown.psu.edu/sites/default/files/posters/clothes-are-not-trash-clothing-recovery-college-campus-nations-capital.pdf)). Carbon emissions impact the environment in various (largely negative) ways such as warming the planet, acidifying the oceans, and destabilizing weather patterns ([Science Insights](https://scienceinsights.org/what-do-carbon-emissions-do-to-the-environment/)).
